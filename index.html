60 day return policies and how they actually work

Most returns come back in the first two weeks, so the extra month costs the retailer little and removes hesitation at checkout. The exclusions are where it gets interesting.

On this page

    A 60 day return policy is a competitive decision, not a legal one. It exists because the data says most returns happen in the first two weeks anyway, so the extra month costs the retailer very little and removes hesitation at checkout.

    Why retailers offer twice the legal minimum

    Return rates cluster hard at the start. Industry reporting has repeatedly shown that the large majority of returns come back inside the first 14 days, with a long thin tail after that. Extending a window from 30 to 60 days adds a small number of returns and a measurable lift in conversion, because the buyer stops weighing the risk.

    There is a second effect the retailer is counting on. The longer someone keeps a product, the less likely they are to return it. Psychologists call it the endowment effect, and it is the reason generous windows are not the loss they look like.

    The advertised number is the headline. The exclusions list is the policy. Opened software, used consumables, personalised goods, final sale items and anything with a hygiene seal usually fall outside it regardless of the window.

    Counting the 60 days correctly

    Day 1 is normally the delivery date for online orders and the purchase date in store. The deadline that matters is often the date the return is initiated rather than the date the parcel arrives back, but plenty of policies say the opposite. On a 60 day window this is the difference between posting on day 58 and being refused.

    Gift returns follow the gift receipt date, which is the purchase date, so a present bought in November and opened in December can arrive with half its window already gone. Several large retailers extend holiday purchases into January for exactly this reason.

    Screenshot the policy the day you buy. Retailers change terms, and the version that applies is the one at the time of purchase. A screenshot with a visible date has settled more disputes than any amount of arguing with a chat agent.

    The policy sits on top of your legal rights

    Return windows cover change of mind. Faults are separate and the clock is much longer. In the EU the minimum legal guarantee against defects is 2 years. In the UK the Consumer Rights Act runs up to 6 years in England and Wales for a claim about goods that were not of satisfactory quality. In the US it varies by state and by warranty terms.

    So a 60 day policy expiring is not the end of the conversation if the product broke. The question moves from "can I return this" to "was this of satisfactory quality when sold", and that argument has no 60 day limit.

    For a change of mind on an online order, the statutory route is the 14 day cooling off right, and the voluntary 30 day money back guarantee sits between the two. Anything past 60 days is the retailer choosing to help, and asking politely is genuinely more effective than quoting law that does not apply.

    The other 60 days: notice periods

    Outside retail, 60 days is a standard notice length in three places. Commercial leases commonly require 60 days notice not to renew. Several US states require landlords to give 60 days notice for rent increases above a threshold or for terminating a tenancy of more than a year. Health insurance and employer benefit changes frequently carry a 60 day notice requirement, and COBRA continuation elections in the US run on a 60 day window from the qualifying event.

    Employment sits either side of this. Junior roles use 30 day notice, senior and regulated roles move to 90 days, and 60 appears mostly in management contracts and in countries where notice scales with length of service.

    Getting a late return accepted

    1. Start the return online before the deadline even if you cannot ship immediately. The initiation timestamp is what most systems record.
    2. Keep the original packaging. Several policies allow returns without it but at a reduced refund.
    3. Use the retailer's own label. Returns that arrive without the RMA reference are the ones that go missing.
    4. If you are days past, ask for store credit rather than a refund. Approval rates are much higher and the agent often has authority to grant it without escalation.
    5. If the item is faulty, say so plainly and stop talking about the return window. Different rules, different clock.

    Common questions

    Is a 60 day return policy required by law?

    No. Statutory cooling off rights are much shorter, 14 days in the UK and EU for distance sales, and the US has no general federal right to return. A 60 day policy is the retailer's own offer.

    Does the 60 days start at purchase or delivery?

    For online orders it is usually the delivery date, and for in store purchases the purchase date. Gift returns normally run from the original purchase date, not the date the gift was opened.

    Can I get a refund after the 60 days if the item broke?

    Possibly, but under consumer law rather than the return policy. Legal protection against defects runs for years in the EU and UK, and the argument is about quality rather than change of mind.

    Do I need the original packaging for a 60 day return?

    Most policies prefer it and some require it. Others accept the return but reduce the refund to reflect the lost resale value, which is permitted in many jurisdictions for change of mind returns.
